The Method

A system, not a set of opinions.

Most market commentary is a stream of takes. My work rests on a framework — a disciplined way to price income and profit, find where the market has it wrong, and size the bet to the odds. Here is how it fits together.

Part one · Valuation

AEG — Abnormal Earnings Growth

At the center is a valuation engine built on Abnormal Earnings Growth (AEG). The core insight is old and unforgiving: a business only creates value when it earns above its cost of capital. Reported earnings and dividends can look healthy for years while, underneath, a company earns less than its capital costs — quietly destroying value the whole time. AEG measures that gap directly.

What makes the engine rigorous rather than rhetorical is that it ties the four major valuation approaches together and forces them to agree:

Abnormal Earnings Growth · Residual Income · Free Cash Flow to Equity · Free Cash Flow to Firm — reconciled to machine precision. If they don't tie, something in the inputs is wrong, and the model says so.

Every valuation runs through hard provenance and completeness gates: the data has to be traceable and internally consistent before a number is trusted. It is the opposite of a back-of-the-envelope multiple. The point is to know why a price is right or wrong — not just to assert it.

This framework is the working extension of my book, Year's Purchase: A History of How Income and Profit Have Been Priced — a history of exactly this question, carried into a tool I use every day.

Part two · Mispricing

Why the market is usually late

Fair value is only half the job. The other half is understanding why a mispricing persists — and when it snaps. A few models do most of the work:

The optimistic fringe. Every security is held, at the margin, by the people most optimistic about it — the ones who assume a building risk won't reach their holding until it demonstrably does. That's why prices levitate while a threat grows, and why the repricing is sudden when it finally arrives.

The marginal buyer and seller. Price is set at the margin. So I ask what the marginal participant is failing to price, and what would force them to.

Actors' incentives, modeled in turn. For macro and geopolitical risk, I reason through each player separately — their red lines, their domestic constraints, their least-bad option — before converging on the path of least resistance.

Reward-to-risk, always. I don't offer point forecasts dressed as certainty. Every call is an asymmetric bet: a wide range of paths to a large payoff versus a narrow path to loss — with the falsification condition stated out loud.

Part three · The evidence

When reported numbers lie

Four companies the crowd priced for stability — while an AEG lens showed value quietly draining away. Each was a "safe" holding right up until it wasn't.

IBMFinancial engineering

Years of buyback-driven earnings-per-share while revenue shrank. Headline EPS held up; economic profit did not. The market kept paying for a growth story the abnormal-earnings math had already contradicted.

GEHidden leverage

A revered industrial whose returns leaned on financial-services leverage and conglomerate accounting. Reported strength masked capital earning below its cost — until the dividend, and the story, broke.

AT&TValue-destroying M&A

Debt-funded media acquisitions that lowered returns on capital while a fat dividend kept yield-seekers comfortable. The abnormal-earnings picture was negative long before the cut and unwind.

Kraft HeinzOverstated brand value

A cost-cut roll-up whose brand assets were worth far less than the balance sheet claimed. A massive write-down and dividend cut simply caught reported figures up to economic reality.

Illustrative case studies of the framework, not investment recommendations. The pattern is the point: reported earnings and dividends can mask value destruction that abnormal-earnings analysis surfaces early.
